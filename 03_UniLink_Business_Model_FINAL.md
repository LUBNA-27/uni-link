### Proposed term sheet (not a signed agreement)

- Paid 30-day pilot: JOD 500, credited against the total deployment fee only on conversion. No production rides before regulatory, insurance, security and Jeeny integration approval. If approval is delayed, begin in shadow mode (planning only; no actual dispatch). 
- One-time implementation: JOD 2,000 inclusive of the JOD 500 pilot credit (JOD 1,500 remainder upon acceptance). One defined corridor, agreed integration interfaces, deployment configuration, tests, documentation and training. Exact scope to be determined jointly; additional work quoted separately.
- Recurring license and maintenance: JOD 150/month after production acceptance, for the agreed module, routine bug fixes, limited support hours, compatibility and minor updates. Proposed business-hours support, not an unfunded 24/7 guarantee. Define SLA, support cap, and escalation in the contract. Jeeny pays/hosts production cloud, dispatch, maps, AI and payment infrastructure directly, or separately reimburses documented usage. Enterprise rollout and other corridors separately scoped.
- Commercial structure: Jeeny operates the platform and retains trip fares/commission subject to approved terms and driver payout. UniLink receives its implementation fee and software subscription from Jeeny. No additional direct fee to students or drivers in initial contract.
- Initial license: commercial use within agreed scope, with founders retaining their underlying code/IP to the extent they own it. Jeeny retains customer data, brand and operational systems. Respect event agreement, university/third-party rights, privacy and software licenses. Before claiming ownership check the official hackathon FAQ/terms and get written clarification. [Event website](https://nextstop.jeeny.me/).
- Acquisition alternative: if Jeeny wants to own the source and IP outright, negotiate a separate acquisition/assignment price after due diligence, scope and IP validation; ongoing maintenance would be a separate service contract if desired. No public acquisition price for Daily or UniLink supports a valuation today. A pilot/license is the ask on stage, not a demand for an acquisition.

### Jeeny economics: actual pilot data needed

Student pays approved fare -> Jeeny settles approved driver compensation -> Jeeny keeps net platform contribution after variable costs. Jeeny pays UniLink for software separately.

Jeeny incremental monthly contribution = contribution from new completed rides + measured retention savings or other verified incremental benefits − lost contribution from displaced existing rides − discounts, payment, dispatch and additional support costs not already counted.

Monthly recurring break-even is at least JOD 150 in incremental contribution to cover the license alone; first 12 paid months require JOD 3,800 total (average JOD 316.67/month), before Jeeny's integration and other incremental costs. Derive required rides using actual incremental contribution per new vehicle booking: ceiling(monthly cost / contribution per booking). Do not use passenger seats as vehicle bookings or present unverified Jeeny internal margins. If contribution is zero/negative there is no trip-volume break-even on that basis. Track cohorts to see if a group trip replaces multiple existing solo Jeeny bookings; more seats do not automatically increase Jeeny revenue.

## 6. Evidence that the solution works — today's real prototype evaluation

Distinguish three categories: (1) actual problem research; (2) observed software/user tests today; (3) future real transportation outcomes, not yet established.

**A. Real price/route benchmark.** For 3–5 consented general pickup landmarks, screenshot current Jeeny Economy quotes in the official consumer app without booking; record time/date, origin/destination and vehicle type; collect students' actual recent fare receipts or self-reports, and bus fare including feeder costs. Run UniLink for equivalent routes and schedules with observed inputs; label calculated group fare as simulated quote, ETA as model prediction (not measured trip arrival). Use approved routes only; no need to book multiple actual vehicles. Distinguish distance from a routing engine from actual driven distance and peak multipliers from actual traffic data. Use 3–5 independent comparable examples, not only favorable ones.

**B. Usability mini-test.** Ask 5–10 peers who did not build the app to plan a commute, understand Best-Deal changes, confirm a simulated trip, and identify if a suitable accessible route is unavailable. Start a timer on first interaction; mark completed independently, assisted, or failed; log duration and issues. Ask if they'd use it (separately from successful task completion). Show actual counts and median time after test, no fabricated results.

**C. Engineering proof.** Run pytest and save raw output; check schedule parsing on distinct redacted screenshots against manually labeled ground truth, then test matching, no-match, capacity, gender/accessibility hard constraints, fare conservation, Wallet no double charge, no accessible option and Best-Deal consent. Capture actual parser/route latency (e.g. median and p95, with n), error rate and fallback status. Benchmark synthetic 10/15/35-rider scenarios separately from real volunteered inputs. A passed software test is not proof of real on-time transport, actual safety, actual pickup within 10 minutes, or commercial viability.
